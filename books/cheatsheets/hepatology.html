🟡 Hepatology On-Call Cirrhosis & Jaundice — Guide for the Duty Physician

⚡ Cirrhosis & Jaundice — At a Glance

A practical framework for the physician on call: how to think about jaundice, how to grade cirrhosis severity fast, and what actually needs urgent attention overnight.

🩺 The on-call mindset

Most calls about a cirrhotic patient fall into a short list of scripts. Recognizing which script you're in changes everything that follows:

🩸 GI bleeding
🧠 Confusion / altered mental status
🌡️ Fever + ascites
🫘 Rising creatinine
🩹 Bleeding / bruising before a procedure
🟡 New jaundice
First question for every one of these: is this a decompensating event (bleed, infection, new AKI, worsening encephalopathy) rather than an isolated abnormal number? Decompensation in cirrhosis is rarely a single-system problem — treat the whole patient, not the lab value.

Common causes of cirrhosis

  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD/NAFLD)
  • Chronic viral hepatitis (B, C)
  • Autoimmune hepatitis
  • Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) / primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
  • Hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α1-antitrypsin deficiency
  • Cardiac / congestive hepatopathy
Typical decompensating triggers
  • Infection (SBP, pneumonia, UTI, cellulitis)
  • GI bleeding
  • Dehydration / diuretic overuse
  • Constipation
  • Sedatives / opioids / benzodiazepines
  • Non-adherence to lactulose or diuretics
  • New nephrotoxin or NSAID exposure
  • Alcohol relapse, portal vein thrombosis, HCC
Jaundice — the 30-second triage
Total bilirubin ↑ │ ├─ Direct (conjugated) ↑↑ → hepatocellular / cholestatic │ → LFTs + imaging (US) │ └─ Indirect (unconjugated) ↑↑ → hemolysis / Gilbert → haptoglobin, LDH, retic count
Red flags requiring immediate escalation: hematemesis/melena in a known or suspected varix carrier, new confusion with asterixis, temperature + worsening ascites, rapidly rising creatinine, or any active bleeding with markedly deranged coagulation before an urgent procedure. These are covered in dedicated tabs — this page is built to be used during the call, not just read beforehand.

🟡 Jaundice — Diagnostic Approach

Classify by bilirubin fraction first (unconjugated vs. conjugated), then by LFT pattern (hepatocellular vs. cholestatic) — this two-step sort gets you to the right differential fast.

Bilirubin metabolism — where the block is
Heme breakdown → Unconjugated bilirubin (indirect) │ (bound to albumin, water-insoluble) ▼ Hepatocyte uptake + conjugation (UGT1A1) │ ▼ Conjugated bilirubin (direct) — water-soluble │ ▼ Biliary excretion → gut → stercobilin (stool color) → urobilinogen → urine
↑ Unconjugated = production (hemolysis) or uptake/conjugation problem (Gilbert syndrome, Crigler-Najjar) — upstream of the liver's excretory machinery. Normal urine/stool color, no dark urine.
↑ Conjugated = hepatocellular injury or biliary obstruction — downstream problem. Dark urine, pale stool possible.
Pre-hepatic

Mechanism: excess bilirubin production or impaired conjugation, normal excretory pathway.

  • Hemolysis (autoimmune, mechanical, hemoglobinopathy)
  • Ineffective erythropoiesis
  • Gilbert syndrome (very common, benign)
  • Crigler-Najjar syndrome

Workup: CBC/smear, haptoglobin (↓), LDH (↑), reticulocyte count (↑), direct Coombs.

Hepatocellular

Mechanism: hepatocyte injury/death — ALT/AST rise disproportionately to ALP.

  • Viral hepatitis (A, B, C, E)
  • Alcohol-associated hepatitis
  • Drug-induced liver injury
  • Ischemic hepatitis ("shock liver")
  • Autoimmune hepatitis
  • Acute decompensation of cirrhosis
Cholestatic / post-hepatic

Mechanism: impaired bile flow — ALP/GGT rise disproportionately to ALT/AST.

  • Choledocholithiasis
  • Malignant biliary obstruction (pancreatic, cholangiocarcinoma)
  • Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)
  • Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
  • Drug-induced cholestasis
  • Sepsis-associated cholestasis
R-ratio — quantifying the pattern
R = (ALT ÷ ALT_ULN) / (ALP ÷ ALP_ULN)
R valuePattern
R ≥ 5Hepatocellular
R ≤ 2Cholestatic
2 < R < 5Mixed
Initial on-call workup
  • Total + direct bilirubin, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, albumin, INR
  • CBC (cytopenias suggest portal hypertension/hypersplenism)
  • Abdominal ultrasound with Doppler — first-line imaging, assesses ducts, liver texture, ascites, portal vein patency/flow
  • If cholestatic pattern + dilated ducts → MRCP or CT to define the obstruction level
  • If hepatocellular pattern → viral serologies, autoimmune markers, medication/toxin history, acetaminophen level if any suspicion
Isolated hyperbilirubinemia with normal ALT/AST/ALP and normal direct fraction in an otherwise well patient is very often Gilbert syndrome — reassurance and outpatient follow-up, not an overnight workup.

These two scores drive most downstream decisions: procedural risk, transplant priority, prognosis discussions, and how aggressively to chase a decompensating event.

What Child-Pugh is (and isn't) good for
  • Simple, bedside, no calculator needed in a pinch
  • Guides general prognosis and rough procedural risk
  • Weakness: ascites/encephalopathy grading is subjective; not used for transplant allocation
What MELD-Na is used for
  • Transplant waitlist priority (the actual allocation purpose)
  • Objective, lab-based, reproducible
  • Score capped 6–40; values outside physiologic bounds are truncated (see formula note)
  • Rising MELD-Na over serial measurements matters more than one snapshot
Neither score should be the sole basis for a bedside decision. A Child-Pugh A patient can still have a life-threatening variceal bleed; a high MELD-Na patient may still tolerate a well-planned procedure with the right precautions. Use these to communicate severity and trend, not as a substitute for examining the patient in front of you.

💧 Ascites & Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis

Mild — detectable only by ultrasound.

Moderate — symmetrical abdominal distension, clinically detectable.

Large / tense — marked abdominal distension, often uncomfortable, may impair breathing.

Interpretation: ≥1.1 g/dL — consistent with portal hypertension
SAAG ≥ 1.1 g/dL (portal hypertensive)
  • Cirrhosis (most common)
  • Cardiac ascites / constrictive pericarditis
  • Budd-Chiari syndrome, portal vein thrombosis
  • Massive hepatic metastases
SAAG < 1.1 g/dL (non-portal hypertensive)
  • Peritoneal carcinomatosis
  • Tuberculous peritonitis
  • Pancreatic ascites
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Serositis (autoimmune)
🚨 Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

Diagnosis: ascitic fluid PMN (neutrophil) count ≥ 250 cells/mm³, in the absence of a surgical/secondary source of infection.

Suspect in any cirrhotic with ascites and:

  • Fever, abdominal pain/tenderness
  • New or worsening encephalopathy
  • Unexplained AKI or hypotension
  • GI bleeding (perform diagnostic tap even without other symptoms)
Low threshold to tap. SBP can present with minimal or no localizing symptoms — a low threshold for diagnostic paracentesis in any decompensating cirrhotic with ascites is the standard of care.
Treatment
  • Empiric antibiotics: third-generation cephalosporin (e.g. ceftriaxone) pending culture; broaden if nosocomial/prior resistant organisms
  • IV albumin: 1.5 g/kg on day 1, 1 g/kg on day 3 — reduces incidence of hepatorenal syndrome and improves survival, especially if bilirubin > 4 mg/dL or creatinine > 1 mg/dL
  • Repeat paracentesis at 48h if inadequate response to confirm PMN decline
  • Avoid nephrotoxins, NSAIDs, and non-selective beta-blockers during active SBP/AKI
SBP prophylaxis
IndicationRegimen
Prior SBP episode (secondary ppx)Long-term norfloxacin or ciprofloxacin/TMP-SMX
GI bleedingShort-course prophylaxis (e.g. ceftriaxone) regardless of ascites
Low ascitic protein (<1.5 g/dL) + additional risk factors (renal/liver failure)Primary prophylaxis may be considered
Large volume paracentesis
  • Therapeutic tap > 5 L requires albumin replacement
  • Rule of thumb: 6–8 g albumin per liter removed beyond 5 L
  • Reduces post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction
  • Hold diuretics if hypotensive or AKI present

🩸 Variceal Bleeding — Acute Management

The single highest-stakes overnight call in a cirrhotic patient. A structured, simultaneous approach (resuscitate + pharmacotherapy + antibiotics + arrange endoscopy) saves time that matters.

Assume variceal source in any cirrhotic with hematemesis or melena until proven otherwise — start pharmacotherapy before endoscopy confirms the diagnosis, not after.
Simultaneous initial bundle (first 15–30 minutes)

1. Resuscitate

  • Two large-bore IVs
  • Restrictive transfusion — target Hb ~7 g/dL (not higher)
  • Avoid over-transfusion (raises portal pressure, worsens bleeding)

2. Vasoactive drug

  • Octreotide (bolus + infusion), or
  • Terlipressin where available
  • Start immediately, continue 2–5 days

3. Antibiotics

  • Prophylactic ceftriaxone (or per local resistance pattern)
  • Reduces rebleeding, infection, and mortality — give regardless of ascites

4. Airway & endoscopy

  • Consider airway protection if massive hematemesis / altered mental status
  • Endoscopy within 12 hours of presentation
Transfusion targets — why restrictive
Over-transfusion ↓ Portal pressure ↑ ↓ Rebleeding risk ↑
  • Target Hb ~7 g/dL (individualize for active massive bleeding, cardiac disease, hemodynamic instability)
  • Correct severe thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy only per active bleeding and planned intervention — see Coagulopathy tab
If bleeding is refractory / uncontrolled
  • Balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore / Minnesota) — temporizing bridge only, 24h max
  • Self-expanding esophageal stent — alternative bridge in some centers
  • TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) — definitive rescue for refractory or recurrent bleeding
  • Early/pre-emptive TIPS within 72h considered in high-risk patients (e.g. Child-Pugh C, or B with active bleeding at endoscopy) at centers with the capability
Primary prophylaxis (known varices, no bleed yet)
  • Non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol, nadolol, or carvedilol), or
  • Endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL)
  • Choice depends on varix size, decompensation status, local expertise
Secondary prophylaxis (after a bleed)
  • Combination: non-selective beta-blocker + EVL — more effective than either alone
  • Repeat EVL sessions until variceal obliteration
  • Consider TIPS if rebleeding despite combination therapy
Beta-blockers and refractory ascites/AKI: non-selective beta-blockers may need to be held or reduced in the setting of refractory ascites, hypotension, SBP with AKI, or hyponatremia — the so-called "window hypothesis." This is a nuanced, individualized decision, not an automatic stop.

🧠 Hepatic Encephalopathy

Before labeling any confused cirrhotic as "just HE" — rule out the mimics and find the precipitant. HE is very often a symptom of something else that needs its own treatment.

West Haven grading
GradeClinical features
0 (minimal)No detectable changes; subtle deficits only on psychometric testing
1Trivial lack of awareness, shortened attention span, mild asterixis or tremor, sleep pattern reversal
2Lethargy, disorientation to time, obvious personality change, asterixis present
3Marked confusion, disorientation to place, somnolent but rousable, muscular rigidity
4 (coma)Unresponsive to verbal/painful stimuli
⚠️ Rule out mimics before anchoring on HE
  • Hypoglycemia
  • Intracranial hemorrhage (cirrhotics fall, bleed, and have coagulopathy — low threshold for CT head)
  • Sepsis / meningitis
  • Alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
  • Wernicke encephalopathy (give thiamine before/with glucose in at-risk patients)
  • Post-ictal state
  • Sedative/opioid accumulation
Find the precipitant — it's almost always there
New/worsening HE ↓ Why? ├── Infection (incl. SBP — tap the ascites) ├── GI bleeding (check rectal exam / Hb) ├── Constipation ├── Dehydration / over-diuresis ├── Electrolyte disturbance (Na, K) ├── Sedatives / opioids / benzodiazepines ├── Non-adherence to lactulose └── Renal dysfunction, TIPS placement
Treatment — lactulose
  • Titrate to 2–3 soft bowel movements per day
  • Oral/NG route preferred; lactulose enemas if unable to take orally or ileus
  • Over-titration causes dehydration/hypernatremia and can itself worsen HE — reassess if excessive diarrhea
Treatment — rifaximin
  • Added for recurrent HE despite adequate lactulose, or secondary prophylaxis after a hospitalized episode
  • Used in combination with lactulose, not as monotherapy
Protein restriction is outdated. Do not restrict dietary protein to manage HE — adequate protein intake (including in HE) is now standard; restriction worsens sarcopenia and outcomes without controlling HE better.
Airway consideration: Grade 3–4 HE with an unprotected airway and risk of aspiration may need ICU-level monitoring or intubation for airway protection — this is a clinical/respiratory decision independent of the HE-specific therapy above.

🫘 Hepatorenal Syndrome & AKI in Cirrhosis

Not every rise in creatinine in a cirrhotic is HRS — and treating it as HRS before excluding other causes delays effective therapy.

ICA-AKI staging (based on creatinine change)
StageCreatinine criteria
1↑ ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48h, or ≥1.5–2× baseline
2Increase 2–3× baseline
3Increase >3× baseline, or Cr ≥4.0 mg/dL with an acute rise ≥0.3, or initiation of renal replacement therapy
Step 1 — stop diuretics, exclude the obvious
  • Hold diuretics and any nephrotoxins/NSAIDs
  • Volume challenge with albumin (1 g/kg/day, up to 2 days) to exclude prerenal/hypovolemic AKI
  • Rule out infection (SBP, UTI, pneumonia) — tap the ascites
  • Renal ultrasound to exclude obstruction
  • Urinalysis/microscopy, urine sodium — exclude ATN (muddy brown casts, high urine Na)
HRS-AKI diagnostic criteria (after exclusions above)
  • Cirrhosis with ascites
  • AKI per ICA criteria, no improvement after 2 days of diuretic withdrawal + albumin
  • Absence of shock
  • No current/recent nephrotoxin exposure
  • No macroscopic signs of structural kidney injury (no significant proteinuria/hematuria, normal renal ultrasound)

HRS-AKI is a diagnosis of exclusion — reached only after the above steps, not a first-line label.

Treatment of confirmed HRS-AKI
Terlipressin (or norepinephrine where terlipressin unavailable) + Albumin ↓ Splanchnic vasoconstriction → effective arterial volume ↑ → renal perfusion ↑
  • Terlipressin + albumin is first-line where available; norepinephrine infusion (ICU setting) + albumin is the main alternative
  • Monitor for terlipressin-associated ischemia and volume overload
  • Involve nephrology/hepatology early — renal replacement therapy and transplant evaluation may be needed if refractory
  • Liver transplantation is the only definitive cure for true HRS
Distinguish HRS from ATN: HRS classically shows a bland urine sediment and low urine sodium (avid sodium retention); ATN shows muddy brown granular casts and higher urine sodium. Overlap exists, especially after an ischemic or septic insult — treat the most likely driver first and reassess.

🩹 Coagulopathy & Procedures

The most consequential misconception in cirrhotic patients: that an elevated INR means the patient is "auto-anticoagulated" or bleeding-prone in a way that must be corrected before any procedure.

Rebalanced hemostasis: cirrhosis reduces both pro- and anti-coagulant factor synthesis in parallel. INR reflects only the procoagulant side and does not reliably predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis — routine correction of a mildly/moderately elevated INR with FFP before low-risk procedures is not supported and exposes the patient to volume overload without proven benefit.
What INR does and doesn't tell you
  • Does not reliably predict procedural bleeding risk in cirrhosis
  • Does not need routine correction with FFP before low/moderate-risk procedures (e.g. paracentesis, thoracentesis, routine central line)
  • Still relevant as a marker of synthetic liver function (used in Child-Pugh/MELD)
  • Global assays (thromboelastography/ROTEM) better reflect overall clot formation when available and are increasingly preferred to guide transfusion before higher-risk procedures
Practical approach before a procedure
  • Match correction strategy to bleeding risk of the specific procedure, not to the INR number alone
  • Avoid routine prophylactic FFP for paracentesis/thoracentesis
  • Use TEG/ROTEM-guided transfusion where available for higher-risk interventions
  • Correct fibrinogen if <100–120 mg/dL and active/high-risk bleeding planned
  • Involve hepatology/anesthesia/IR early for high-risk procedures in advanced disease
Thrombocytopenia
  • Common — from hypersplenism (portal hypertension) and reduced thrombopoietin
  • Platelet transfusion generally reserved for active bleeding or very low counts (roughly <50,000/µL) before higher-risk procedures — thresholds vary by institution/procedure
  • Thrombopoietin-receptor agonists (e.g. avatrombopag, lusutrombopag) are an option pre-procedure in select patients to avoid transfusion
Portal vein thrombosis
  • Cirrhotic patients are not protected from thrombosis despite elevated INR — PVT occurs
  • Consider in acute decompensation, new/worsening ascites, or abdominal pain
  • Anticoagulation may be indicated in selected patients (especially transplant candidates) — individualized, specialist-guided decision
Vitamin K is reasonable to trial (won't harm, may help if there's a nutritional/cholestatic component) but rarely normalizes INR when the elevation is due to synthetic liver failure itself — don't rely on it as a bridge to an urgent procedure.

AST:ALT ratio > 2 is classically suggestive of alcohol-associated liver injury (though not specific).

Drugs to use cautiously or avoid
  • NSAIDs — avoid; worsen renal perfusion and GI bleeding risk, can precipitate HRS
  • Benzodiazepines / opioids — use cautiously, lowest effective dose; can precipitate or mask hepatic encephalopathy
  • Acetaminophen — not contraindicated, but consider reduced max daily dose (commonly ≤2 g/day) in advanced cirrhosis
  • Aminoglycosides — heightened nephrotoxicity risk, avoid if possible
  • IV contrast — weigh AKI risk, ensure adequate hydration, don't withhold if diagnosis genuinely requires it
  • ACE-inhibitors/ARBs — often held in decompensated cirrhosis/ascites due to hypotension and renal perfusion concerns
Nutrition
  • Do not routinely restrict protein — sarcopenia worsens outcomes and is common in cirrhosis
  • Target adequate protein intake (roughly 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day in most decompensated patients, per dietitian/hepatology guidance)
  • Late-evening snack / avoid prolonged overnight fasting — reduces catabolism
  • Thiamine before/with glucose in any patient with alcohol use history (prevent Wernicke encephalopathy)
🧮 CIWA-Ar — Alcohol Withdrawal Assessment

Relevant in alcohol-associated cirrhosis — withdrawal is itself a common precipitant of decompensation and can mimic or compound hepatic encephalopathy. Score each item, total updates automatically.

Total CIWA-Ar: 0 / 67
Interpretation: Minimal / no withdrawal — monitor
In cirrhotic patients, treat withdrawal carefully: benzodiazepines remain first-line but accumulate in liver failure — prefer shorter-acting agents (e.g. lorazepam, oxazepam, which don't require hepatic phase-I metabolism) and use symptom-triggered (CIWA-guided) rather than fixed-schedule dosing where feasible.

Interpretation: –
If DF ≥ 32 (severe)
  • Consider corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone) if no contraindication (active infection, GI bleeding, renal failure)
  • Screen for and treat infection before starting steroids
  • Reassess response with the Lille score at day 7 — a poor Lille score identifies non-responders in whom steroids should be stopped
Related scores (reference)
  • Glasgow Alcoholic Hepatitis Score (GAHS) — alternative severity index incorporating age, WBC, urea, PT ratio, bilirubin
  • MELD — also used/validated for alcoholic hepatitis severity in many centers (see Cirrhosis Severity tab)
  • Lille score — day-7 steroid response; guides continuation vs. discontinuation of steroids
